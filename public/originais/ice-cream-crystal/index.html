Why one scoop is silky and the other icy

Ice cream is about half ice by volume. What you feel on your tongue is not how much ice there is but how big its crystals are. Below about 40 µm (four hundredths of a millimetre) they are too small to notice and the scoop feels smooth; above about 50 µm you feel them as grit.

What the churn does

The mix freezes against the cold wall of the bowl. The colder the wall, the faster new crystals appear there: the rate climbs steeply with how far below its freezing point the mix is pushed. The dasher scrapes that fresh ice off the wall twice a turn and stirs it through the mix, so millions of seeds share the ice that forms later. The same amount of ice split among more crystals means smaller crystals. The dasher also beats in air, which is why churned ice cream is lighter.

Left still, the mix grows a shell of ice from the wall inward. The shell insulates, the freezing front slows down, few new crystals start, and the ice that forms later grows onto the ones already there: big crystals, an icy scoop. A tub of mix put straight into a home freezer is the extreme case.

Why it gets icy in your freezer

Every time the freezer warms a little (the door opens, the defrost cycle runs), the smallest crystals melt first; when it cools again the water freezes onto the biggest ones. Over weeks the small ones disappear and the big ones grow. A smooth batch turns icy; a colder, steadier freezer slows it down.

What you can do

Freezing speed: drag the slider on the console (or in the card), press its snowflakes, or 1 2 3 and [ ]. The cyan snowflake is liquid nitrogen. Churn: click the motor or the dasher to stop and start it, drag the big gear to change its speed, or press space. Zoom in: click a scoop or its bubble, or press ⇧Z: crystals, air cells and fat droplets drawn at their true relative size. Store it: the card's freezer slider, or ⇧F.

What is exact and what is not

The numbers come from a small crystal-population model of the batch: heat leaving through the wall, crystals starting at the wall at a rate that rises with the fourth power of the undercooling, all crystals growing in step with the ice that forms, the smallest melting first. Its constants are chosen so the results sit in the ranges reported for real ice cream; the churn is a showroom model with a glass bowl so you can watch (a real one is steel), and the magnified bubbles draw fat droplets one in a few hundred.
