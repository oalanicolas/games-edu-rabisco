Why the coast breathes

The Moon pulls on every bit of the Earth, but not equally: the ocean on the near side a little harder than the Earth's centre, the ocean on the far side a little less. Measured from the centre, both sides are stretched away from it, so the water settles into two bulges, one under the Moon and one opposite, with a shallow belt between them.

The bulges stay lined up with the Moon while the Earth turns underneath. A coast rides through a bulge, the belt, the other bulge and the belt again: two high waters and two low waters a day. The Moon moves on along its orbit meanwhile, so the Earth needs 24 h 50 min to face it again, and high water comes every 12 h 25 min, about fifty minutes later each day.

What you are looking at

The glass is the ocean's surface in the equilibrium tide, the textbook shape: the height is A · (3cos²θ − 1)/2, with θ the angle from the point under the Moon and A = (m/M)(R/d)³·R. For the Moon A is 0.36 m, so from high to low is only 0.54 m; the Sun adds the same shape towards itself, 0.16 m. The glass is drawn 5.2 million times taller, measured from the lowest water. The two little coasts are markers riding the globe: the lighthouse's sea and the cove's float rise and fall with the height under them, and the console's tide clock, the wall panel and the chart draw their tides against time.

Things to try

Drag the globe sideways to turn it under the Moon (or drag the chart) and watch the lighthouse's water. Drag a coast anywhere, or tap the globe to put the cove there. Tap the tide clock on the console (or press ⇧T) to run one day fast: two highs, two lows. Slide the phase or drag the Sun round its ring: at new and full moon the Sun's bulges line up with the Moon's (spring tides); at the quarters they cross (neap tides). Drag the Moon up or down: north or south of the equator, one bulge passes nearer a mid-latitude coast than the other and the day's two highs come out unequal; in the far north, one high a day.

What is real and what is not

The rhythm is real: the 12 h 25 min beat, the daily delay, the inequality when the Moon stands north or south, springs and neaps every half month. The local clock is not: real oceans are basins that slosh and resonate, so the tide sweeps round each basin (amphidromic systems) and arrives at very different times and heights. Real ranges run from a few centimetres to 16 m in the Bay of Fundy. The equilibrium tide explains why the coast breathes, not exactly when yours will.
