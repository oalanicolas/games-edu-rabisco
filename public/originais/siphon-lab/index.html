How a siphon lifts water with no pump

Fill a tube with water, put one end in a tank and let the other end hang lower than the tank's surface. The water runs out of the low end, and more water climbs up out of the tank to replace it, over a wall if one is in the way. Nothing pushes it but gravity.

Why it flows

The tube holds two columns of water joined at the top. The leg that goes down to the outlet is taller than the leg that climbs out of the tank (counted from the tank's surface), so it weighs more and falls, and it cannot fall without pulling the short leg after it. The difference in height between the tank's surface and the outlet is the head. The water leaves at v = √(2 g · head / (1 + K)), where K adds up the losses: the entrance, the bends and the friction along the tube.

What holds the column together

At the top of the arch the water is pulled from both sides, so its pressure there drops below the air's: the gauge on the brass fitting shows it. The air outside, pressing on both tank surfaces, is what keeps the water up in the short leg. That sets the limit: the air can hold up about 10 m of water, so a crest more than about 10 m above the tank's surface makes the water boil at the top (at room temperature, at 2.3 kPa) and the column breaks. At this size the crest dips only a few kPa, far from that.

The three things to try

The outlet. Drag the lower tank (or its brass outlet) up and down, or use the slider. Below the waterline, water flows; the lower it goes, the faster. As the tanks level out the flow slows and stops. Lift the outlet above the waterline and the flow stops and runs back: with the outlet in the air, the column drains back into the upper tank and the siphon loses its prime. With the outlet under water, the siphon simply runs backwards.

Prime. A siphon only works full. The tube starts full of air; the priming bulb at the outlet draws the air out and the water climbs the short leg, crosses the crest and fills the long one. Then the bulb can stop: gravity takes over.

The air valve at the crest. Open it and air is sucked in, not water squirting out, because the pressure inside is below the air's. Bubbles ride down the long leg and make it lighter, the flow falters, the air gathers at the top, and the column splits there: each leg falls back into its own tank.
