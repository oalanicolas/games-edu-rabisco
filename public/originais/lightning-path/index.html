Why a strike forks

The flash is the finale. Before it, a stepped leader of negative charge creeps down from the cloud in short jumps: in real lightning about 50 m at a time, with a pause of some 50 µs between them. At every step it can grow from any point of the channel it has built so far. It tends to grow where the electric field is strongest, but not always there. So it forks: the leader is a search, and every branch is a path it tried.

What you are looking at

The chamber holds one slice of a storm cell, scaled so the cloud's base stands 1 km above the floor. The glowing lines are the electric field. Each one carries the same share of charge, so they crowd wherever the field is strong. The faint contours are equipotentials, one every twelfth of the cloud's potential. The violet channel is the leader, bright where it is still growing and fading where a branch gave up. The amber channel rising from the mast is its upward leader: once the field just above the tip passes 400 kV/m, the mast sends its own leader up to meet the one coming down. When they touch, the return stroke races up the joined channel from the ground, at about a third of the speed of light. That is the flash, and the branches it did not use go dark.

Why the mast bends the vote

A tall, narrow conductor gathers the field lines onto its tip: the first number in the card says how many times stronger the field is there than over open ground. So the mast answers first and reaches farthest. A broad plate at the same height (Plate) spreads the lines instead: over its flat top the field rises only because the gap above it is shorter, and when it is struck, it is struck at the rim. Side by side (Both), each takes the leaders that come down on its side, but the point does it from a far smaller spot.

Things to try

Drag the mast along the floor, or drag its ball to make it taller or shorter, and watch the field follow. Click the mast to swap it for a plate, then for both. Turn the cloud knob (or the slider): a stronger cloud lets the mast launch its upward leader from farther away. Press the bolt knob, or Strike, for a new leader; the hourglass slows it right down so you can watch each step. The wave knob turns the thunder on. The same slice runs in all three views: the storm cell pulled apart, and the strike alone in the studio.

What is exact and what is not

The field is Laplace's equation, solved on a 140 × 150 grid (each cell 10 m), with the cloud's base held at the cloud's potential and everything grounded at zero. The leader grows by the dielectric-breakdown model of Niemeyer, Pietronero and Wiesmann (1984): each step picks a free cell next to the channel with a chance proportional to the field there raised to the power 1.4. It is a two-dimensional slice, so the mast behaves like a thin wall seen end-on; a real rod concentrates the field even more. The voltages and heights are real-world sizes, but the mast is a very tall tower (560 m, taller than the Empire State Building) so its pull reads, and time is slowed: a real leader's descent takes a few milliseconds, the return stroke tens of microseconds.
