How the prism room works

A lamp sends a thin sheet of white light into a 60° prism of N-SF11, a dense flint glass. Every ray on the bench is traced through the real glass, one colour at a time: 24 wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm.

Why the colours split

Light slows down in glass, and it bends where it crosses a surface at an angle (Snell's law). Glass slows violet a little more than red: N-SF11's refractive index is 1.845 at 400 nm and 1.772 at 700 nm. So violet bends a little more at each face, and after two faces the colours leave in slightly different directions. White light was all of them together.

Turn the prism

Drag the prism (or the dial on the console, the card's slider, [ ]). The whole fan swings: it climbs as you turn one way, stops, and falls back. The top of that climb is minimum deviation, where the light crosses the glass parallel to its base. Turn too far the other way and the colours can no longer get out of the second face: total internal reflection traps violet first, then the rest.

Tilt the light

Drag the lamp along its arc (or the card's Light slider, , .). A steeper beam hits the glass more obliquely: the fan leaves flatter and a little narrower. Compare the spread in the third number.

Bring the colours home

Press ⇧P (or the knob on the console's right, or click the second prism). An identical prism, turned upside down, slides in right behind the first. Together they act like a thick slab of glass: each colour is bent back by exactly what the first prism did, so they leave parallel and on top of each other, and the stripe on the screen is white again. Drag it along the beam to pull the pair apart, or turn it: the colours no longer overlap and coloured fringes come back. This is Newton's own experiment.

What is exact

The glass (Schott's Sellmeier fit for N-SF11), Snell's law at every face, the reflection losses, total internal reflection, and every angle and number on this page. The ends of the spectrum are drawn brighter than your eye sees them, and the haze that makes the beam visible is theatre.
