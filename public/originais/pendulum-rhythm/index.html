How the pendulum lab works

Two pendulums hang from one steel gantry: blue, the longer one on the left, and amber, the shorter one on the right. They are let go together from the same angle. A pendulum's rhythm is set almost entirely by its length: one swing (there and back) takes 2π√(L/g), so a pendulum four times as long takes twice as long. The shorter one is a faster runner on the same track: it pulls ahead a little every swing, the two drift out of step until they swing as mirror images, and then it laps the other and they are in step again. The ring between them shows exactly that: each pendulum is a dot running round it, and they meet when the dots do.

The sweet spot

They fall back into step every 1 ÷ (1/T₁ − 1/T₂) seconds, whatever the lengths. Only when the two rhythms make a simple ratio, like 5 swings against 4 or 3 against 2, do they meet again exactly where they started, every time. The lengths click into those ratios when you pass them. Surprisingly, the closer two lengths are, the longer they take to meet again.

What you can do

Change a length: drag a bob up or down its rod, turn a knob on the console (click a knob to jump to the next sweet spot), or use the sliders. The rig catches both pendulums and lets them go together when you let go. Drag a bob sideways to pull both back further before the release. Move through time: drag the tape on the console (the playhead stays in the middle, like a tape head), click a marker, or use the chevrons to jump to the previous or next time in step. The glowing trace in the frame is each swing drawn against time: the past to the left of the centre line, the prediction to the right. Push: click or flick a bob. A push shifts that pendulum's rhythm (and, because bigger swings are a little slower, its tempo too), so every meeting after it moves. Scrub back before the push and it happens again; push in the past and the future is rewritten.

Wave hangs fifteen pendulums tuned to 51, 52 … 65 swings a minute. Released together, they fan out into travelling waves, split into two, three and four groups, look like chaos, and all line up again after exactly one minute.

What is exact, and what is not

The swing is the full nonlinear pendulum, θ″ = −(g/ℓ)·sin θ − c·θ′, integrated four hundred and eighty times a second, with the sphere of the bob counted in its length and a very light air drag. Bigger swings really are slower (a 16° swing takes about 0.5 % longer than a tiny one). Every time in step on the timeline is found from that same simulation, not from a formula. Not modelled: the rods' and hubs' own weight (taken as light), and the frame is rigid (on a flexible beam, as Huygens found with two clocks in 1665, pendulums slowly pull each other into step). Lengths read as a tabletop rig: 1 unit on screen is 10 cm.
