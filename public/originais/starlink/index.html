    How the internet reaches a house in Iceland

    The house is in north-east Iceland, 65° north. Its dish is bolted to the top of the east gable on Starlink's own wall mount, and it never moves. The dots above are the real Starlink satellites, most of them around 460 km up, each one travelling at 7.6 km/s and going right round the Earth in about 95 minutes. The bright line is the link to the one satellite the dish is talking to at this instant.

    Why it changes satellite

    At that speed a satellite is high enough in your sky (above 25°) for four and a half minutes at most, and this far north usually less: a pass tends to end while the satellite is still well up, as it leaves the part of the sky the tilted dish can steer to. Before it goes, the dish picks another one coming up and moves the conversation across. The counter is the time left on the current one. Starlink's real scheduler reshuffles every 15 seconds, so a real dish changes more often than this one; here each satellite is kept for its whole usable pass so a hand-off can be watched.

    Why nothing moves

    The dish is a phased array: 1,200 small antennas under a flat cover, half a wavelength apart. Each fires the same signal a fraction of a cycle after its neighbour. The waves only add up in one direction, and that direction is set by the delay: no delay points straight out of the face, a quarter of a cycle between neighbours points it 30° to the side. Drag the dish face to aim the beam yourself. Point it off the satellite and the link drops; press Auto to give the steering back to the dish.

    Why it is quick

    Latency is mostly distance. Straight up to a satellite overhead and straight back down is 930 km: 3.1 ms at the speed of light, 6.2 ms for a round trip. Add the slant of a real pass, the laser hop to a ground station, the fibre behind it and the processing, and a ping from this house comes home in about 35 ms. A geostationary satellite sits 35,786 km up: the radio alone needs 477 ms for the round trip even straight overhead, and about 600 ms in practice.

    Lasers, trees and rain

    Iceland has no Starlink ground station of its own. So the satellite over the house passes each packet to another on a laser link (violet), and that one sends it down to a gateway in Tromsø, on the Isle of Man or in Ireland. Switch the lasers off and most of the time the packet has nowhere to go: only now and then is a satellite placed to see both the house and Tromsø, 1,587 km away. Light in vacuum covers 300,000 km a second; in glass fibre only about 200,000 km, so a long hop through space can beat the cable underneath it. The Ku and Ka bands are absorbed by water, so a wet tree in the beam costs the link for as long as the satellite is behind it, and a downpour costs about 1.8 dB per kilometre of rain the beam crosses, which is worst when the satellite is low.

    Where the satellites come from

    Every dot is a real satellite. They are 10,681 Starlink spacecraft from the public element set published on 1 October 2026, each one carried forward from its own epoch by Kepler's laws plus the two drifts the Earth's bulge causes. That is accurate to a few kilometres over the hours this page ever winds through, which is far inside anything it claims. It is not SGP4, though, and after a few days it would be wrong. The shells are real too: four inclinations, at a spread of altitudes, which is why they cross each other instead of forming one tidy lattice. The lights on the night side are real places, about 3,500 towns and cities, brighter where more people live.

    What is simplified

    The Earth's own turn is left out, and the orbits are treated as circles. Latency adds a fixed allowance for processing. The house, the dish, the satellites, the Starship and the gateways are all drawn far larger than life so you can see them at all, and the README says by how much. The ground is laid level for 300 km round the house, so the house has somewhere flat to stand. The Starship's orbit is invented, since there is no Starship in the snapshot, and so are the satellites peeling off its dispenser. The distances, speeds and light-times are computed, not typed in.
