Why the window corner feels cold

This is a living room cut in half so you can see its air. The glowing ribbons are the air itself, moving as a live simulation of the room's cross-section works it out: blue where it is cold, orange where it is warm.

What you see

The window glass is much colder than the room ( on its inner face when it is outside). Air touching it cools, gets heavier and slides down the glass. At the sill it spills onto the floor and runs across the room as a shallow cold current: right past your ankles on the sofa. The radiator does the opposite: it warms the air around it, which rises, spreads under the ceiling and comes back to the window to be chilled again. One big loop, and you sit in its cold half.

What you can change

Open the window gap (lift the lower sash, the fan key on the console, or 1 2 3): outdoor air pours in at the sill as a cold jet and joins the current along the floor. Move the radiator (drag it, the console's slider, or [ ]): park it under the window and its warm plume meets the cold fall head-on and lifts it, the loop turns round and the floor current is warm and slow. That is why radiators live under windows. Show the temperature field (the thermometer key, the thermostat on the wall, or ⇧T) to see the cold layer the ribbons are carrying. Drag the probe on the floor to measure anywhere.

The draught number

The probe reads air speed and temperature 10 cm above the floor, where ankles are most sensitive, and turns them into the draught rating of the comfort standard ISO 7730: the share of people who would complain about the draught. Under 10 % is a comfortable room, over 20 % is a room people move out of.

What is exact and what is not

The air is a real (two-dimensional) fluid simulation: buoyancy, the glass's heat loss, the gap's inflow and the radiator's heat, with a thermostat holding the room at . A slice cannot spread sideways the way a real room does, so the model loses some momentum to a slow drag; the speeds land where rooms like this are measured. The ribbons run faster than the air so you can follow them, and the gap and the frost are drawn larger than life.
