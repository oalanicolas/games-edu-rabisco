How a skate turns

A figure-skate blade is about thick and not flat underneath: it is ground with a shallow hollow (a radius) that leaves two sharp edges, and along its length it is curved, a rocker of about radius, so only a few centimetres touch the ice at once.

Lean, and the rocker becomes a turn

Stand the blade upright and its rocker curves up and down, not sideways: the blade runs straight. Lean it by θ and part of that curve turns sideways; seen from above, the edge touching the ice now traces an arc of radius R / sin θ. The deeper the lean, the tighter the arc. To hold a lean on that arc without falling, a skater needs the speed that makes tan θ = v²/(g·r): here.

The bite

On an edge the whole load rides on one corner. The ice gives way until the contact is big enough to carry it at the ice's yield pressure (about ), so a deeper lean sinks the edge deeper and longer: a groove a few tenths of a millimetre deep and several centimetres long. The groove's wall is what holds the skate sideways. Flat, the blade sits on both edges, barely deep; there is no wall to push against, so a turn forced on a flat blade skids and scrapes snow out of the turn.

What is drawn larger than life

The skate is drawn larger than the ice it rides: the rink is a patch of ice about across at 1 unit to 2 m, so the carve lines on it are true to scale, while the skate stands at its own scale in the middle. Its blade and heel are drawn about 1.3 cm taller than a real one's so the steel shows under the boot. The contact glow is drawn at the skate's scale, the bite in the cross-sections is exaggerated, and the spray is drawn for looks.
