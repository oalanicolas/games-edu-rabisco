How a hummingbird hovers

In the glass tunnel is a robotic hummingbird wing, about seven times life size, slowed down a hundred times so that one wingbeat, about a forty-fifth of a second, takes a couple of seconds. Smoke drifts through the tunnel to show the air.

It sweeps, it doesn't flap

A hovering hummingbird doesn't beat its wings up and down. Each wing sweeps forward (the downstroke) and back (the upstroke) in a nearly flat plane, through about 120°, and the tip draws a sideways figure eight. The console's film strip shows one beat; its slider scrubs through it.

The twist

At each end of the sweep the wing turns over at the shoulder. Going forward it meets the air leading edge first, tilted up about 45°. Going back, it has spun round so the same edge leads, still tilted up. So the air is thrown down on the way back as well, and the upstroke lifts too. Set Twist to None and the wing keeps its forward tilt on the way back: now it pushes itself down, and almost all the lift is gone.

Why the way back does less

Measured on real hummingbirds, the downstroke carries about three quarters of the weight and the upstroke a quarter. In this model that comes from three things: the wing doesn't turn over quite all the way, its curve (camber) is shaped for the downstroke and upside down on the way back, and the sweep is tilted a little, forward and down, so drag helps on the way forward and hinders on the way back. Full twist shows an insect-like, even stroke.

Beat faster, lift more

Lift grows with the square of the speed, so it grows with the square of the beat rate. About 45 beats a second holds this 3.3 g bird up; fewer and it sinks, more and it climbs. The curve on the console is the lift through one beat, amber forward and cyan back, against a line at the bird's weight.

What is exact and what is not

Forces come from a quasi-steady model: at each instant the wing is treated as a flat plate at its angle, with lift and drag coefficients measured on a robotic fly wing. It leaves out the extra kicks at the turns (rotational lift, wake capture). The smoke and vortices are a drawn flow field shaped by the model's lift, not a fluid simulation. The robotic wing is a showroom model, not a hummingbird's anatomy.
