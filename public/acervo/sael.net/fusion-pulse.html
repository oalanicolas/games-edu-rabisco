How pulsed fusion pushes back

Most fusion machines try to hold a plasma steady and boil water with its heat. This one works in pulses, about one a second, and takes its electricity straight off the magnets. Each pulse lends the magnets energy from a bank of capacitors, squeezes a plasma until it fuses, and then lets the plasma push the magnets' field back out. That push drives current back into the capacitors, the way an electric car's motor turns into a generator when it brakes. It is the approach Helion is building in Everett, Washington.

1 · Charge

The energy waits in a bank of fast capacitors charged to tens of thousands of volts (Helion's Polaris has more than 50 MJ). Not supercapacitors or batteries: those hold plenty but take seconds to empty, and a pulse needs its energy in millionths of a second. Switches open the bank into the coils; whatever the coils give back flows into the same capacitors.

2 · Form

Deuterium and helium-3 gas at each end is turned into plasma, and the coils there flip their field. The plasma ends up carrying a ring of current that makes its own field, pointing the opposite way inside to the field outside. That is a field-reversed configuration, or FRC: a self-contained ring of plasma with its field lines closed on themselves, nearly all pressure and almost no field of its own, so it pushes on the magnets almost as hard as they push on it.

3 · Launch and collide

Rings of coils fire one after another down each side, like a coilgun, and throw the two plasmoids at each other at 447 km/s (1.0 million mph). Their momentum is equal and opposite, so when they meet it cancels: all of their motion, 0.42 MJ, becomes heat, and the merged plasma starts at about 17 million °C.

4 · Squeeze

The middle coils take the field from 0.9 T to 15 T. Field pushes like a gas: at its peak the magnetic pressure is 90 MPa, nearly 890 atmospheres. The plasma shrinks to 29 L and heats as it is squeezed, like air in a bicycle pump, its ions reaching 117 million °C. Its electrons start cool and catch up by colliding with the ions, which costs the ions heat.

5 · Burn

Hot enough, a few nuclei get through each other's repulsion and fuse. With deuterium and helium-3, every product is electrically charged, so it stays tangled in the field and adds to the plasma's push. With deuterium and tritium, four-fifths of the energy leaves as neutrons, which fly straight through the coils as heat in the walls and push nothing.

6 · Push back

The coils let go. The plasma expands, shoving the field back out through the windings; a changing field through a coil drives a current (Faraday's law), and that current runs back through the switches into the capacitors. This pulse lends 33 MJ and gets 30 MJ back (90 %).

7 · The ledger

Coming out ahead means fusion has to push back more than everything the pulse loses: the switches, cables and coils (Helion aims for 95 % round trip), the field fading while it is held, the heat the plasma leaks, the heat it still carries when it leaves, and any neutrons.

Where Helion is (October 2026)

Polaris, the seventh prototype (19 m long, a 50+ MJ bank, fields over 15 T), started running at the end of 2024. In January 2026 it became the first private machine to show deuterium-tritium fusion, with plasma at 150 million °C. Helion now describes Polaris's goal as demonstrating electricity from fusion, and is building Orion in Malaga, Washington, to sell Microsoft 50 MW from 2028. Making more electricity than a pulse uses has not been shown yet.

What is exact and what is not

The fusion rates are the standard Bosch and Hale fits, the energy per reaction is exact, the squeeze is adiabatic in pressure balance with the field, the electrons share heat at the textbook (Spitzer) rate, and every joule is accounted for: what the bank lends equals what comes back plus every loss. The machine's sizes, the plasma's confinement and the circuits' efficiency are this page's assumptions, set near Helion's published numbers; it is a simplified model, not Helion's design or data. The pulse is shown in slow motion, each phase stretched by its own factor (the squeeze takes 60 millionths of a second), and the plasma and coils are drawn about twice as wide as they would be at the machine's length.
