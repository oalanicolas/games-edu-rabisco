How the maglev works

This is a short test track with one carriage cut open, so you can see the two ways a train can float and the two feedback loops that keep it where it should be.

Attract: pull up from under the rail

In the Attract setup (the German Transrapid's way), arms on the carriage reach round the rail and hold electromagnets under it. The magnets pull themselves up towards the steel, so the carriage is lifted. The pull grows as the square of the current and falls as the square of the gap, F = k·(N·I)² / g², so the balance is unstable: rise a millimetre and the magnet pulls harder, so it snaps shut; sink a millimetre and it pulls less, so the carriage drops. A gap sensor and a controller trim the current hundreds of times a second to hold about 10 mm. Switch the Height loop off and try to balance it with the current yourself.

Repel: push off the track

In the Repel setup (Japan's SCMaglev), superconducting magnets ride above coils in the track. Moving over them, they induce currents whose field pushes back, so the lift grows with speed. Sink and the push grows; rise and it weakens, so this balance is stable by itself, at a gap of about 10 cm. Standing still there is no lift at all, so the carriage rolls on wheels until it is going fast enough.

Side guides

Lifting is only half of it. Guidance magnets on each arm pull the carriage towards the rail's sides, and like the lift magnets they are unstable, so position sensors (the posts at each end) feed a second loop that keeps it centred. Switch the Side guides off and a breath of side wind is enough: the carriage drifts until its emergency skid scrapes the rail.

What is exaggerated

The gaps are drawn about 20 times too big in Attract and 5 times in Repel, time runs 20 times slower in Attract (5 times in Repel) so the controller can be seen at work, and the carriage is a stubby showroom model. The forces are for a 50-tonne carriage.
